Mackenzie’s MCAT Study Hub

  1. Home
  2. Projects
  3. Mackenzie’s MCAT Study Hub
Full-Stack Web AppLive · private

A private study planner I built for my girlfriend Mackenzie as she prepares for the MCAT — one calm place for her plan, her calendar, her notes and her flashcards.

Mackenzie’s study material lived in a spreadsheet, a notes app and a pile of tabs. I wanted her to open one thing on her iPad each day and know exactly what to do next. The hub turns her 25-week plan into a daily dashboard, a calendar, a rich-text notebook, and a flashcard system with written-recall quizzes — all synced to her account across devices and installable like an app.

It started as a deliberately small brief: a single-student planner with a checklist, an editable 13-week plan, notes and score history. It grew as she used it: her real plan is 25 weeks, and flashcards and written quizzes became first-class features. It is a private tool for one person, so the screenshots below show her plan and study notes only — no scores or personal records.

Features

  • Daily dashboard — what’s up next in the plan, a weekly hours target (60 minutes Mon/Wed/Fri plus weekends, Tue/Thu off), a countdown to the exam date, today’s checklist, and a Monday-to-Sunday roadmap with weekly progress.
  • Calendar — week and month views with per-day completion counts, monthly hours planned, and one-tap session creation.
  • Study plan — 25 editable weeks grouped into phases (Content review, Practice & review, Official-style practice, Taper & recover), imported from her original spreadsheet and editable week by week.
  • Notes — a rich-text editor with headings, lists, checklists, four highlight colours and autosave, with notes linkable to a study session.
  • Flashcards and quizzes — decks, a daily “study due” queue, written-recall quizzes, and review history.
  • Scores and resources — a benchmark score tracker and a short list of free study resources.

Difficulties and how I solved them

1. Keeping a private study life private

Problem. The hub holds Mackenzie’s plan, notes and (eventually) practice scores. A hosted Firebase app is reachable by anyone, so “it’s just for her” has to be enforced by the database, not by hoping nobody finds the URL.

Solution. Everything lives under her user ID, and Firestore security rules deny all access by default, allowing a read or write only when the signed-in user owns the path. I tested the rules against the Firebase emulator for the cases that matter — the owner is allowed, a different signed-in user is denied, and a signed-out visitor is denied — so privacy is checked rather than assumed. No admin credentials or open rules ship with the client.

2. Turning a spreadsheet into an editable plan

Problem. Mackenzie’s real plan was a spreadsheet: rows of weeks, date ranges, topics and daily tasks. It needed to become something she could change on an iPad without touching code — and her plan was 25 weeks, not the 13 the first brief assumed.

Solution. I imported the sheet into structured weeks (title, date range, topics) grouped into phases, kept a reference back to the source rows, and made every week editable in the app. The data model treats the plan as content she owns, so the number of weeks and the curriculum can change without restructuring the app.

What building it taught me

Cloud

  • Shipped a production web app on Firebase: Authentication for private sign-in, Cloud Firestore for per-user synced data, and Firebase Hosting for the HTTPS deployment.
  • Designed data around the signed-in user so one person’s plan, notes and scores stay private to her account.

AI-assisted engineering

  • Ran the build as a spec-driven, AI-assisted project: a phased implementation plan with acceptance checks per phase, explicit non-goals, and a starter prompt that gave the coding agent guardrails (one phase at a time, tests before merge, no scope creep).
  • Treated the agent as a fast collaborator, not an autopilot — I owned the product decisions, reviewed each phase, and steered when the real plan outgrew the original brief.

System and product design

  • Scoped a deliberately small single-user product, then extended it based on real use: a 13-week brief became a 25-week plan, with flashcards and written quizzes added.
  • Designed for the device she actually uses: an iPad-first, installable layout with large touch targets, a bottom tab bar, and clear saved/loading states.
